Plane card vs boarding pass

Four takes on making the two cards from one catch read as two different objects. The sample catch is Air Canada AC 412, an Airbus A220-300, Toronto YYZ to Montréal YUL (507 km). Each take shows the pair, then the reveal deck: the plane in front, the pass peeking out behind it.

A

Shiny card, paper pass

The plane is a metal-framed trading card with a navy name plate; the flight loses the frame and becomes thin white paper with notched corners, a red airline rule and a tear-off stub.

The pair
The reveal deck
B

Window seat, bag tag

The plane is seen through a cabin window on sky-blue stock with round cockpit-style dials; the flight is a die-cut baggage tag with a punched hole and a huge destination code, so the shapes differ even before you read them.

The pair
The reveal deck

In the deck the tag's string loops up above the stack, which marks it as the flight at a glance.

C

Photo print, ink pass

The plane is a white-bordered photo print held by metal photo corners (the tier) with the catch date burned in; the flight is a navy-topped pass, so one card is light and the other dark-headed at a glance.

The pair
The reveal deck
D

A real boarding pass

The flight stops being a playing card: it's a wide paper ticket with a tear-off stub, QR code and pass-style fields (the gate and seat slots hold your catch: when, how high, the set and the rarity as the boarding group), with the plane's photo printed on it.

The ticket
The reveal deck · the ticket tucked behind the plane card (take A's)

The ticket is wider than the card, so its airline band, YYZ → YUL and the stub's QR stay visible on three sides of the plane card. In the Collection it would sit in a wide row of its own rather than in the card grid.
